It cites a source for every claim

Each row carries where it came from, the date it was last read, and how far we trust it. Anything unconfirmed looks unconfirmed:

Guaranteed by state law Published policy Needs confirming

A student who acts on a wrong transfer-credit claim loses real money and a real semester. So the app is built to be checkable rather than convincing.

And it is allowed to say “I don't know.”

In Texas we have not confirmed exam mappings against each campus's published policy. So the conservative route comes back empty and says so: “there is nothing here we would stake your money on.”

We could have made Texas look as strong as California by relaxing one confidence level. The whole thing would have been worth less.

The rule nobody tells you

Texas

Finish the 42-hour core at any Texas public college and the receiving university must substitute it for its own. That is statute, not policy.

Florida

An Associate in Arts guarantees admission to a state university with junior standing — though not to the campus you want. We say both halves.

Ohio, Illinois, Michigan…

25 states carry a statewide transfer rule in the app today. It applies to every public campus at once, which is why no single campus page mentions it.

Everywhere else

The other 26 get the truth — we have not confirmed yours — plus what is national anyway, and the offer to tell you when that changes.

Also: the cheap credit, and the trap in it

Sophia, Study.com, Saylor, StraighterLine, TEEX, Modern States — the cheapest credit in America. The app lists what each costs, who recommends it, and whose transcript the credit lands on.

That last one is what decides whether it is worth anything. Your college is not asking “was that course any good?” — it is asking “do we accept this company's paperwork?”. The University of California answers no, in writing, for all of them.
